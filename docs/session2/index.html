Recap of the reinforcement learning (RL) model

On each trial the model compares what happened with what it expected, and moves its expectation a fraction of the way towards the outcome:

$$\underbrace{\delta_t}_\textrm{prediction error} = \underbrace{o_t}_\textrm{outcome} - \underbrace{p_t}_\textrm{model prediction} \tag{Equation 1}$$
$$\underbrace{p_{t+1}}_\textrm{new prediction} = \underbrace{p_t}_\textrm{old prediction} + \underbrace{\alpha \delta_t}_\textrm{scaled prediction error} \tag{Equation 2}$$

$o_t = 1$ if option 1 (green) was rewarded and $0$ if option 2 (orange) was; $\alpha$ is the learning rate, with $0 < \alpha \le 1$.

Option 2 is rewarded whenever option 1 is not, so its probability is $1 - p_t$: the model only needs to track one probability.

The learning rate captures the speed of learning

By comparing the speed of learning ($\alpha$) in the volatile and the stable phase, we can test our first hypothesis: how fast we learn and adjust our behaviour depends on how quickly the environment is changing.

Expanding the model: integrating probabilities with magnitudes

The model tracks a probability. The task also shows points. Something has to put them together into a single number that choices can be based on.

$$\underbrace{u}_\textrm{utility} = \underbrace{m}_\textrm{reward magnitude} \times \underbrace{p}_\textrm{reward probability} \tag{Equation 3}$$

or, less optimally, a weighted sum

$$\underbrace{u}_\textrm{utility} = \overbrace{\omega}^\textrm{magnitude weight} \times \underbrace{m}_\textrm{reward magnitude} + \overbrace{(1-\omega)}^\textrm{probability weight} \times \underbrace{p}_\textrm{reward probability} \tag{Equation 4}$$

Expanding the model: from utility to a choice

A utility for each option is still not a choice. The model needs a decision rule that turns the difference between the two utilities into the probability of picking one of them.

That probability is what we will later compare against what a participant actually did, which is how the model gets fitted at all.

A deterministic rule

If $u_1 > u_2$, always choose option 1.
If $u_1 < u_2$, always choose option 2.

The step sits at the indifference point, $u_1 = u_2$, where the two options are worth exactly the same and the rule has nothing to go on.

Discuss with each other
  • Is a deterministic decision rule the best one in our experiment, and why?
  • How would the experiment need to change to make it a bad rule?

A stochastic rule: the softmax

If $u_1 > u_2$, choose option 1 with a higher probability — the bigger the difference, the higher. A logistic function gives this S-shape:

$$\underbrace{P(c_1)}_\textrm{probability of choosing option 1} = \frac{e^{\beta u_1}}{e^{\beta u_1} + e^{\beta u_2}} = \frac{1}{1+ e^{-\beta(u_1 - u_2)}} \tag{Equation 5}$$

What $\beta$ does

$\beta$ is the inverse temperature. The higher it is, the steeper the curve, and the less often the model takes the worse option.

The name is an allusion to thermodynamics: a high temperature, meaning a low $\beta$, means particles moving at random, so behaviour is noisier.

Why might recovery or falsification fail?

  • Too few trials — say 20 stable and 20 volatile
  • Too little or too much variance in the relevant variables — only stable blocks, or blocks that switch every 5 trials

Either way you find out before collecting a single participant, which is the whole point of doing it first.

Palminteri et al., TiCS, 2017 · Scholl & Klein-Flügge, Behavioural Brain Research, 2018 · Lockwood & Klein-Flügge, SCAN, 2020

Simulating our hypothesis as false

Simulate every participant with a single $\alpha$ across both blocks, then fit the two-learning-rate model to the result.

If the fitting invents a difference that was never there, a difference in the real data would mean nothing.

Simulating our hypothesis as true

Now simulate with two learning rates, $\alpha_S$ in the stable block and $\alpha_V$ in the volatile one, and fit the same model.

If the difference does not come back out, the task cannot measure the thing the whole study is about.

Expanding the model: simulating choices

To simulate a choice, compare the choice probability with a random number between 0 and 1: option 1 if the number is lower, option 2 if not. Over many trials the model then chooses option 1 exactly as often as its choice probability says.

What makes a good fit?

A good fit assigns a high choice probability to the choices the participant actually made.

So for a given $\alpha$ and $\beta$ we can compute, trial by trial, how likely the model thought the observed choice was — and multiply those together across trials.

Discuss
  • Why might multiplying hundreds of probabilities together be a problem on a computer?

The (negative) log likelihood

$$\underbrace{\log L}_\textrm{log likelihood} = \sum_{t} \log \underbrace{P(c_t \mid \alpha, \beta)}_\textrm{probability the model gave the observed choice}$$

Taking logs turns the product into a sum, which stays representable. Fitting maximises this, or equivalently minimises its negative, the error.

Discuss
  • Which trials dominate the sum, and why is that a good thing?

Grid search: the likelihood landscape

Why not always use grid search?

  • It spends most of its time computing the error in parts of the parameter space that are obviously wrong
  • It becomes intractable with many parameters: if two parameters give 100 combinations, four give 10,000
  • There are ways to find the maximum without measuring every single point
  • Those ways can get stuck in a local maximum, so they are run from several starting points

One intuitive trick: gradient ascent

Start somewhere, work out which way is uphill, take a step, repeat. This is what the notebook's fitting routine does — from ten starting points, keeping whichever run ends highest.
