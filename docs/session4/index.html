Recap: the log likelihood

$$\underbrace{\log L}_\textrm{log likelihood} = \sum_{t} \log \underbrace{P(c_t \mid \alpha, \beta)}_\textrm{probability the model gave the observed choice}$$

Maximise this, or multiply by $-1$ and minimise the negative log likelihood instead — the same thing.

Recap: how do we fit? Why not grid search?

  • Most of the work goes into parts of the parameter space that are obviously wrong
  • It becomes intractable quickly: 100 combinations for two parameters is 10,000 for four
  • There are ways to find the maximum without measuring every point
  • Those ways can get stuck in a local maximum, so they are run from several starting points

One intuitive trick: gradient ascent

Start somewhere, work out which way is uphill, take a step, repeat — from ten starting points, keeping whichever run ends highest.

Models that are not nested: the BIC

$$\textrm{BIC} = k \log (n)- 2 \log (\textrm{likelihood}) \tag{Equation 8}$$
  • A trade-off between goodness of fit and the number of parameters $k$
  • Smaller is better
  • No p-value: a heuristic, not a test
Discuss
  • What did the BIC suggest?

Model comparison: pros and cons

  • AIC $= 2k - 2 \log (\textrm{likelihood})$ can overfit, and prefer models more complex than they need to be
  • BIC $= k \log (n) - 2 \log (\textrm{likelihood})$ penalises complexity more as the sample grows — already more than AIC once $n \geq 8$ — so it usually prefers simpler models, and sometimes underfits
  • Both are crude but a reasonable start. Try both; they often agree. When they do not, it is a judgement call — plot the parameters and the fits
  • If two models have the same number of parameters, compare log likelihoods directly, or use a likelihood ratio test if they are nested
  • Cross-validation is another option, not covered here

Akaike, 1973 · Schwarz, 1978 · O'Hagan & Forster, 2004 · Burnham & Anderson, 2002 · Vrieze, 2012

Model frequency and exceedance probability

Each participant is allowed their own model, rather than all of them sharing one.

The frequency is the share of participants a model accounts for; the exceedance probability is the chance it is the most common one of the four.

Stephan et al., NeuroImage, 2009

Report writing

Turn to your neighbour and discuss
  • What were the main experimental hypotheses?
  • Did we find evidence for them?
  • Did anything unexpected happen?

If you cannot answer these three in a sentence each, the report has no spine yet. Start here, not at the abstract.

Abstract

  • Very short — under 300 words
  • No more than two sentences on each of: hypothesis, methods, results, implications

Introduction

  • Keep it short — 200 to 400 words
  • What each experimental question is, and why it matters
  • How you will test it, and what you predict the outcome will be
  • At most four citations

Methods

This is a key section.

  • Participants, exclusions, numbers
  • Task design, including a figure
  • The computational model, including its equations
  • Which parameters you derived, and how they test your hypotheses
  • Statistical tests and model comparison

At every point, do not just list what you did — explain why and how.

Results

  • Follow the same structure you introduced in the methods
  • For each result, briefly recap what the analysis was for
  • You can make most of the figures you need from today's notebook — but add figure numbers, titles, legends and descriptions
  • Report tests in full: for a t test, the degrees of freedom, the t value, the p value and an effect size

Discussion

  • 1000 to 1500 words
  • Start with a short summary of your findings
  • What do the results mean? Were they what you expected?
  • How do they relate to the previous literature? How is the Blain and Rutledge paper different from the Behrens paper?
  • Limitations of the data and analyses, and future analyses: with more time, what would you have liked to analyse, and why?
